## Introduction

Offline reinforcement learning increasingly relies on large, reward-free trajectory datasets to pretrain a single reusable policy — a _foundation policy_ — that can solve an open set of goals in a zero-shot manner. This idea underlies recent large-scale efforts from unsupervised RL pretraining to Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that train one policy on heterogeneous data and deploy it across many unseen tasks. IEL contributes a new theoretical and algorithmic foundation for this setting: it is the first approach to jointly satisfy the three properties the authors argue are essential for a foundation policy to handle **long-horizon, multi-stage tasks** — unsupervised (goal-agnostic) training, _directional_ distances, and the _triangle inequality_.

## Problem Statement

The environment is modeled as a Controlled Markov Process (CMP) $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P})$ with state space $\mathcal{X}$, action space $\mathcal{A}$, and transition kernel $\mathcal{P}$, observed only through an offline dataset of trajectories generated by a fixed behavior policy $\pi\_B$. The goal is to recover the **optimal in-distribution foundation policy** $\pi^\star\_{D,g}$: for any goal state $g$, the policy (restricted to actions supported by the behavior data, $\pi \ll \pi\_B$) that minimizes the _expected hitting time_ $V^\pi(x,g) = \mathbb{E}[T^\pi\_{x\to g}]$ — the expected number of steps to reach $g$ from $x$.

The authors identify a gap in prior offline representation-learning methods, organized into three families (successor measures, reachability distances, hitting-time methods — summarized in **Table 1**):

- Successor-measure {% cite touati2021learning %} and reachability {% cite myers2025quasimetric %} methods largely fail the triangle inequality, preventing local distances from composing into valid multi-stage plans.
- Existing hitting-time methods (e.g., GAS {% cite baek2025graph %}, HILP {% cite park2024foundation %}) use **symmetric Euclidean** features, which cannot represent the inherently **irreversible, directed** nature of most real transitions, limiting their ability to "stitch" together sub-goals into long-horizon plans.
- No prior method satisfies unsupervised training, directional distance, and the triangle inequality simultaneously — the "missing primitive" the paper sets out to fill.

## Methodology

IEL is built on an **operator-theoretic, Hilbert-space representation** of the environment, in the spirit of Koopman/Perron-Frobenius operator theory used for nonlinear dynamical systems:

- States are mapped into a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ via a feature map $\phi: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{H}$, and transitions are approximated by bounded linear operators $\mathcal{K}\_a \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})$, one per action. This is formalized as an **$\epsilon$-Sufficient CMP** (Definition 2.1): $\left\|\mathbb{E}\_{x' \sim \mathcal{P}(\cdot\|x,a)}[\phi(x')] - \mathcal{K}\_a\phi(x)\right\|\_{\mathcal{H}} \le \epsilon$ — a CMP whose dynamics are linearizable in the latent space up to a bounded error $\epsilon$.
- The paper's central methodological result, **Hitting Time Isomorphism**, shows that under this condition the expected hitting time is _exactly a linear functional of the displacement_ between the goal's and the state's latent features: $V^\pi(x,g) = \langle \phi(g) - \phi(x), \omega\_g^\pi \rangle\_{\mathcal{H}}$ for some vector $\omega\_g^\pi \in \mathcal{H}$ — i.e., temporal structure becomes geometric structure.
- This displacement representation is proven to exist under a mild range/adjoint condition ($\mathbf{w\_1} \in \mathrm{range}(\mathcal{K}\_\pi - I)^\ast$) and to be **unique up to a bounded linear isomorphism** $M$, meaning the learned latent geometry reflects the environment's true directed temporal structure rather than an arbitrary embedding.

From this theory the authors derive **Isomorphic Embedding Learning (IEL)**, a concrete algorithm for training goal-agnostic foundation policies from offline data.

## Algorithm

IEL (Section 2.3, pseudocode in Appendix F) has four components:

1. **Task identifier learning** — a contrastive (InfoNCE-style) objective learns a mapping $\omega\_\psi(g)$ from a goal state to a "directional axis" vector, using augmented positive views of goal states against in-batch negatives: $\mathcal{L}\_{\mathrm{NCE}}(\psi) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum\_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{\omega\_\psi(g\_i)^\top \omega\_\psi(\tilde{g}\_i)}{\tau} - \log\sum\_{j=1}^{N}\exp\left(\frac{\omega\_\psi(g\_i)^\top \omega\_\psi(\tilde{g}\_j)}{\tau}\right)\right]$.
2. **Hitting-time regression** — intermediate states along offline trajectories are relabeled as goals, and the model regresses observed temporal gaps (discounted hitting times) using expectile regression (in the style of IQL {% cite kostrikov2022offline %}), keeping targets bounded and stable.
3. **Temporal-difference learning** — because long-horizon Monte Carlo hitting-time targets are rare and heavy-tailed, IEL restricts the interim goal horizon and instead learns via TD backups. The authors show HILP's {% cite park2024foundation %} symmetric $L\_2$ objective is a special case ($\beta = 0$) of IEL's formula, and introduce an exponential penalty term (controlled by temperature $\beta$) on the angular misalignment $\xi$ between the state-goal displacement and the learned task direction, $V^\pi(x,g) \approx \|\phi(g)-\phi(x)\| \exp(\beta(1-\cos\xi\_{x\to g,g}))$ — this is what injects **directional bias** absent from HILP.
4. **Directed graph-based planning** — at evaluation time, IEL builds a directed graph over a diverse coreset of dataset states (selected via a Determinantal Point Process for spatial coverage). Edges are initialized from a symmetrized Minimum Spanning Tree (for connectivity) augmented with $k$-Nearest-Neighbors, but retain **asymmetric costs** from the learned quasimetric:
   $$d(s,t,g) := \|\phi(t) - \phi(s)\|\_2 \cdot \exp\big(\beta(1-\cos(\xi\_{s\to t,g}))\big).$$
   Navigation runs a single-source shortest-path search from the goal on the reversed graph, and the agent greedily follows the resulting path.

Training proceeds in **three phases** (Algorithm 3): (1) train the task encoder via contrastive learning, then freeze it; (2) train the state encoder $\phi$ via TD + hitting-time regression (Algorithm 1), then freeze it; (3) train the actual policy $\pi(a\|x,z)$ with any off-the-shelf offline RL algorithm, conditioned on uniformly random unit-length latent directions $z$ — never on real goals — preserving goal-agnostic training.

## Theoretical Analysis

- **Existence (Theorem 2.2):** the expected hitting time admits an exact linear representation $V^\pi(x,g) = \langle \phi(g) - \phi(x), \omega\_g^\pi \rangle\_{\mathcal{H}}$ if and only if the range condition $\mathbf{w\_1} \in \mathrm{range}(\mathcal{K}\_\pi - I)^\ast$ holds on the adjoint of the policy-integrated transition operator $\mathcal{K}\_\pi$.
- **Identifiability (Theorem 2.3 / Corollary 2.4):** any representation satisfying this hitting-time identity is related to the canonical one by a **unique, bounded linear isomorphism** $M: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{D}^\star$ — the learned geometry is provably a faithful, coordinate-independent recovery of the environment's directed temporal structure, not an arbitrary heuristic distance (unlike HILP {% cite park2024foundation %}/GAS's {% cite baek2025graph %} unconstrained symmetric features, or TMD's {% cite myers2025quasimetric %} quasimetric which lacks this operator-theoretic grounding).
- **Consistency under finite-dimensional compression (Theorem 3.3):** the global hitting-time prediction error is bounded as $\sup\_{x,g} \left\|V^\pi(x,g) - \widehat{V}^\pi\_g(x)\right\| \le \dfrac{C\_H \|\omega\_g^\pi\|\_{\mathcal{H}}\,\epsilon}{1-\rho(\mathcal{T}^\pi\_Q)}$, i.e. the one-step latent transition error $\epsilon$ is **amplified by the factor** $\dfrac{1}{1-\rho}$, where $\rho$ is the transient spectral radius of the compressed transition operator $\mathcal{T}^\pi\_Q$. Practically: small local representation errors compound more severely for goals that are hard to reach (a larger effective planning horizon).
- **Finite-sample guarantees (Theorems 3.4–3.5):** the population minimizer of the regression objective is shown to align with the true reference displacement geometry via a bounded linear map, and a full PAC-style bound decomposes the total finite-sample error into approximation error ($\eta\_\epsilon^\star$), statistical (Rademacher) complexity ($\Gamma\_N(\delta)$), data-coverage ($c\_{\mathrm{cov}}$), and trajectory-label mismatch ($\eta\_{\mathrm{traj}}$) terms.
- Together, these results give IEL a level of mathematical rigor — provable recovery of the environment's directed geometry up to a bounded linear map, with quantified error bounds — that prior distance-based representation methods (HILP, GAS, TMD) do not provide.

## Results

IEL is evaluated on **six offline goal-conditioned RL benchmarks** from the D4RL {% cite fu2020d4rl %} suite: `antmaze-large-diverse`, `antmaze-large-play`, `antmaze-ultra-diverse`, `antmaze-ultra-play`, `kitchen-partial`, `kitchen-mixed`. Baselines are GC-IQL {% cite kostrikov2022offline %}, GC-CQL, and **HILP** {% cite park2024foundation %} (the primary prior-art baseline for foundation-policy training with multi-stage planning), each evaluated with HILP's own recursive-midpoint (Rec-Mid) and symmetric-graph (Sym-Graph) planning. IEL is evaluated with both symmetric ($\beta = 0$) and asymmetric/directional ($\beta = 0.1$) graph planning.

**Table 2** (mean normalized return $\pm$ std, **8** seeds, $10^6$ gradient steps) shows two key outcomes:

- IEL's **symmetric**-graph planning already outperforms HILP's recursive-midpoint planning by a significant margin in **5 of 6** environments — isolating the benefit of graph-based planning alone.
- Adding **asymmetric** (directional) planning gives further significant gains over symmetric IEL in **4 of 6** environments, with only minor degradation in the remaining two.
- Representative numbers: on `antmaze-large-diverse`, IEL Asym-Graph reaches **71.8 ± 6.5** vs. HILP Rec-Mid's **68.0 ± 6.3** and GC-CQL's **36.2 ± 19.0**. On `antmaze-ultra-play`, IEL Asym-Graph reaches **73.8 ± 6.2** vs. HILP Rec-Mid's **56.0 ± 18.9** — the largest margin in the study.

This confirms the paper's central hypothesis: **directional embedding learning and graph-based planning are both necessary and complementary** for effective multi-stage planning in foundation policies.

Additional findings:

- **Robustness (Table 4, Appendix G):** almost all hyperparameter configurations land within **two** standard deviations of the best-performing setting — IEL is not narrowly tuned.
- **Compute:** experiments ran on a single RTX 4090 (**24 GB**) + Intel i7-14700K; a full training-and-evaluation cycle takes **~1 hour**. At inference, asymmetric graph planning costs about the same as HILP's Rec-Mid, while symmetric graph planning is **~3×** faster due to graph reusability.

## Conclusion

IEL is presented as the first foundation-policy learning algorithm to jointly satisfy unsupervised (goal-agnostic) training, directional distance, and the triangle inequality — the three properties the authors identify as necessary for reliable multi-stage planning (Table 1). It is grounded in a new operator-theoretic framework proving that expected hitting times are exact linear functionals of latent displacements, identifiable up to a bounded linear isomorphism, with full finite-sample PAC guarantees. Empirically, it improves on the state of the art (HILP) for offline foundation-policy learning across six D4RL maze and kitchen benchmarks. The authors note open questions for future work — extending the range-condition guarantees to nonlinear/high-dimensional settings, quantifying the effect of state-space discretization, and tightening the TD-learning bound to further strengthen directionality — and frame the broader ambition of the work as helping shift Vision-Language-Action (VLA) research from imitation learning toward reward-based, foundation-policy learning.
